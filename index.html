Learning to Bridge Humanoid Robot Skills

Daniel Gigliotti1
1Sapienza University of Rome
Code

Abstract

Humanoid robots can learn agile skills individually, including walking, jumping and kicking a ball, while many tasks require several skills to be executed in sequence. Each learned skill is reliable only from states similar to those encountered during training. Independently trained skills are therefore not necessarily compatible: for example, a jumping skill that begins from a crouch may not be able to take control from a robot that is walking.

We investigate whether a single learned policy, referred to as a bridge, can bring the robot from the state left by one skill to a state from which the next skill can continue, while all skills remain frozen. The bridge is trained on human motion retargeted to the robot, rather than on executions of the skills. It comprises a diffusion model that plans the transition and a tracking policy that executes the plan. We evaluate the approach in simulation on isolated transitions between skills obtained using different methods, in three longer tasks, and against direct switching and transition policies trained for each skill pair.

Problem Definition

A skill is a policy trained independently and then kept frozen. It succeeds only from a limited set of states, referred to as its initiation set, which depends on its training. During a task, a high-level controller selects the next skill and may request a switch at any time. At that point, the robot is in state A, left by the source skill, which typically lies outside the initiation set of the incoming skill.

We place a bridge between the two skills. When a switch is requested, the bridge takes control and, within a specified duration, brings the robot to a target state B from which the incoming skill can continue. The bridge then hands over control. Targets are states visited during the incoming skill's own executions, from which continued execution has been observed. Because a single state does not specify the robot's motion, the bridge receives a short motion segment on each side of the switch: the final second of the source motion, ending at A, the first half-second of the incoming motion, beginning at B, and the duration. The bridge does not observe the skills themselves and can therefore be shared across skill pairs.

Method

Motion Data

The bridge does not observe the skills during training. Instead, it learns how the robot can move between states from human motion capture data from LAFAN1 and the CMU and KIT subsets of AMASS. We select locomotion and related motions, including walking, running, turning, stopping, crouching and jumping; AMASS clips are selected using BABEL labels. We retarget the motion to the robot with GMR, correct its floor contacts and discard frames that the robot should not be required to reproduce. Because recorded clips rarely contain the transitions required by the bridge, we also use a motion graph to join segments from unrelated clips, for example, a walking segment followed by a jump. Training examples are windows extracted from these clips. Each window provides a history and a future to the bridge, which must generate the intervening motion.

Diffusion Planner and Universal Tracker

The bridge separates motion planning from execution. A planner, implemented as a diffusion model trained on the motion data, receives the request and generates the complete kinematic trajectory between A and B by inpainting the frames between the history and future. Its denoiser is a transformer that represents each frame as a token and predicts changes in the root and joint positions, together with foot contacts. The resulting plan is corrected to end exactly at B.

A universal tracker, a single policy trained with PPO to follow short kinematic trajectories, then executes the plan on the robot. The tracker observes up to 32 control steps ahead; near the end of the bridge, this includes motion from the incoming skill. Its actions provide corrections to the plan's joint positions. The plan is generated once, when the switch is requested, and control passes to the incoming skill when the specified duration expires.

Skills

Every skill is trained independently and kept frozen, and none of them was trained with the bridge in mind. They were deliberately obtained in different ways: some are trained with a task reward alone, some track a reference motion, and some combine the two.

Unitree G1

Walking follows velocity commands and is trained with a task reward. Jumping tracks a 1.54 m forward jump that begins from a crouch. Kicking tracks a human football kick, with additional rewards for ball contact. The four karate skills each track a single strike: lunge punch, front kick, roundhouse kick and side kick. They are extracted from one performer's fight sequence in LAFAN1 and therefore share the same opening and closing guard.

Two additional motion-tracking skills cover rising from a seated position on the floor to standing and sprinting forward. The sprint reference begins with the robot already in motion.

Booster T1

The walking skill advances by a commanded distance and stops at the destination. The turning skill rotates in place to a commanded heading, while the pushing skill moves a box by a specified distance. All three skills are trained using task rewards and are used in the Sokoban task below.

Transitions

Each transition evaluates a single switch between two G1 skills. The same bridge is used for every transition.

Walking, Jumping and Kicking

A target state from the incoming skill is placed ahead of the robot. The bridge is invoked at a specified distance from the target and is given a fixed duration in which to reach it.

Walk to jump

The jump begins from a deep crouch, and the jumping skill has not observed a walking robot during training. The bridge lowers the walking robot into the crouch and halts its forward motion at the take-off location.

Walk to kick

The target lies along the approach to the ball, where the kicking motion is already underway. Although the posture changes little, the robot's speed and supporting foot must match the target motion. The bridge therefore hands over control while the robot is still walking.

Jump to walk

The bridge takes control while the robot is still absorbing the landing and brings it into the walking cycle.

Sit to sprint

The bridge takes control while the robot is still rising from a floor-sitting position and brings it to an already moving sprint entry. The sprint skill then continues without resetting the robot.

Karate

Each strike ends in a guard, and the next strike begins from a stationary guard. The bridge target is a state in the incoming strike's opening guard, positioned at the robot's current location. The bridge brings the robot from the closing guard of the preceding strike to this target.

Comparison with Baselines

We compare the bridge with two baselines:

  • No-op: the incoming skill takes control as soon as the switch is requested, from whatever state the source skill left the robot in.
  • Transition policy: the method of Lee et al., Composing Complex Skills by Learning Transition Policies (ICLR 2019). It also keeps the skills frozen and inserts a learned controller between them, but a separate policy is trained for each pair of skills, on executions of the skills it connects, and the policy decides by itself when to hand over.

Walking, Jumping and Kicking

We measure the incoming skill's performance after handover relative to its performance when started from states in its own executions. At its best distance and duration, the bridge achieves the highest score on all three transitions. The largest difference occurs for the jump-to-walk transition, where neither baseline prepares the walking skill. On the walk-to-jump transition, the transition policy approaches the bridge's performance; on the walk-to-kick transition, all three methods perform well. This performance comes at the cost of a longer transition: the bridge runs for 0.6 to 1.2 s before handover, compared with at most 0.18 s for the transition policies.

Demos

In each demonstration, a high-level controller selects the next skill and requests each switch, which the bridge executes. Because the bridge requires only the switch request, the high-level controller does not need to specify how the transition is performed.

Soccer, Unitree G1

The robot walks toward a fallen robot, jumps over it, proceeds to the ball and kicks it into the goal. A simple controller follows this fixed sequence and places each switch target using geometric rules derived from the scene. The bridge connects the three skills while the robot remains in motion.

Karate, Unitree G1

The robot performs a routine of four karate strikes—a lunge punch, front kick, roundhouse kick and side kick—without being reset. After each strike, the bridge brings the robot from its closing guard to the guard from which the next strike begins.

Sokoban, Booster T1

A classical planner computes a solution before the robot moves. The controller then executes the plan one action at a time using the walking, turning and pushing skills, invoking the bridge at each skill change. In this run, the robot solves the puzzle in approximately 89 s over 23 switches. Because all three skills begin from a nearly stationary state, the bridge primarily aligns the robot—for example, with a box before pushing—rather than enabling otherwise infeasible switches.
