Abstract

Humanoid robots can learn agile skills one at a time: walking, jumping, kicking a ball. A useful task needs several of them in a row, and the weak point is the switch. A learned skill only works from states similar to those it was trained on, and two skills trained independently have no reason to fit together: a jump that starts from a crouch cannot take over from a robot in mid stride.

This thesis studies whether a single policy, the bridge, can make these switches possible without touching the skills. When one skill ends, the bridge takes control and brings the robot, within a deadline, to a state from which the next skill can continue, then hands over. The bridge never sees the skills. It receives the recent motion of the robot, a short piece of the motion the next skill will perform, and the time available, and it is learned from a corpus of human motion retargeted to the robot.

The final bridge, a diffusion planner whose plans are executed by a universal tracker, reaches targets as different as a robot in mid stride, a deep crouch before a jump and an upright walk, without bringing the robot to rest first. In a soccer task on the Unitree G1 it completes all 50 runs, against 4 of 50 when the skills are switched directly.

Frozen skills

No skill is trained again, and no skill knows the bridge exists. Skills can come from task rewards, motion tracking or both.

One bridge for every pair

Transition policies need a network for each pair of skills. A single bridge serves every switch.

Learned from human motion

Trained on about 11 hours of retargeted and stitched human motion, not on rollouts of the skills.

No stop required

Targets can be moving states, such as mid stride in the approach to a kick, so the robot keeps its momentum.

Problem

The switch

A skill is a frozen policy together with its initiation set, the states from which it is expected to succeed. A plan runs skills one after another, and each skill either ends on its own or is interrupted by the high level. When it ends, the source skill leaves the robot in a start state A, which is usually outside the initiation set of the incoming skill. The bridge takes control at that moment and, within a deadline, brings the robot to a target state B from which the incoming skill continues.

The request

A single state does not say how the robot is moving: the same posture can be held still or passed through in the middle of a run. Each switch is therefore described by a request with three parts, and the bridge needs nothing else:

  • History: the last second of the source motion, ending in A. It covers a whole gait cycle, so it shows stride, cadence and phase.
  • Future: half a second of the incoming motion, starting from B. It tells how the robot has to be moving when the bridge hands over.
  • Deadline: the time available, from 0.3 to 1.2 s.

The request has the shape of a motion in-betweening problem: two known pieces of motion with a gap to fill. For training, windows of this shape are cut from recorded motion, where the gap is known. At deployment the history comes from the source skill and the future from the incoming one.

Choosing the target

The best target is the best state of the incoming skill that the robot can reach in time, and finding it is left open. As a proxy, targets are entry points: states that the incoming skill actually goes through in a nominal run, each kept with the 32 states that follow it as the future. The skill continues from an entry point as it does in its own runs, so the target is a state it accepts by construction.

Method

Motion corpus

The bridge is trained on human motion capture from LAFAN1 and AMASS (CMU and KIT subsets, selected with the BABEL labels), turned into training data by a six stage pipeline:

  1. Selection: keep walking, running, turning, stopping, crouching and jumping; drop dancing, fighting and object interaction.
  2. Retargeting with GMR and grounding: fix feet that hover above or sink below the floor.
  3. Kinematic filtering: mark frames the robot should not be asked to reproduce, with limits specific to each robot.
  4. Motion graphs: stitch pieces of unrelated clips, so that a window can start in one motion and end in another.
  5. Windowing: cut history, gap and future from each clip.
  6. Randomization: target displacement, time rescaling, mirroring, observation noise and physics randomization.

Three architectures

All three share the same interface, the request in and joint targets out, and learn from the same corpus. Each one addresses the limits of the previous.

1

Goal-conditioned policy

Trained with PPO. Observes its distance from B and a clock, and is rewarded for following the recorded motion and for reaching B at the deadline.

Learns the average of the motions that realize similar requests, and is not precise enough at the handover.

2

Conditional VAE

Distilled with DAgger from a tracking teacher. A latent variable selects one motion: number of steps, timing, style.

Decoded contacts are not enforced, a single Gaussian prior, and the latent can be ignored when the corpus shows few alternatives.

3

Diffusion planner + universal tracker

A diffusion model plans the whole motion between A and B, and a tracker trained with PPO executes it on the robot.

  • Planner. A transformer denoiser in which every frame is a token. It fills the frames between history and future by inpainting, predicting position changes and foot contacts, and samples a plan with 50 DDIM steps. The plan is rebuilt from A and corrected to end exactly in B. Besides reconstruction, the losses penalize jumps between frames, changes that do not add up to B, and sliding feet.
  • Tracker. A single PPO policy that follows any short kinematic trajectory. It looks up to 32 steps ahead, so near the handover it already sees the future of the incoming skill, and its action is a correction on top of the joint angles of the plan.
  • Deployment. At the interruption the planner generates one plan, the tracker follows it, and at step h control passes to the incoming skill.

Bridge against direct switch

In a direct switch the incoming skill takes control immediately, in whatever state the source skill left the robot. The score compares the incoming skill after the switch with its nominal run from the entry point, so 1 means as good as nominal: velocity error for the walk, tracking error for the jump, ball speed for the kick. Each method is run on the same 50 recorded source executions.

In isolation the direct switch survives, because the incoming skill can start anywhere. It performs much worse, though: right after the landing the walk follows its velocity command with almost three times its nominal error, and a jump started from a walking robot tracks its reference with almost twice the nominal error. In a task, where a skill has to start in a given place, this is what makes direct switches fail (see the soccer demo).

Demos

The demos call the bridge repeatedly inside complete tasks. A high-level controller requests every switch without knowing how it is made, so it can be as simple or as elaborate as the task requires.

Soccer, Unitree G1

Robots fall often during RoboCup matches, and a fallen robot becomes an obstacle that others stumble over. On a 12 by 8 m pitch, the robot has to jump over a fallen robot 3 m ahead, walk to the ball and kick it into the goal: three switches, walk to jump, jump to walk and walk to kick. The controller follows this fixed sequence and places every entry point with geometric rules, for example so that the jump clears the fallen robot and the kick sends the ball toward the center of the goal.

Sokoban, Booster T1

Sokoban is a puzzle on a grid in which a player pushes boxes onto goal cells, and a careless push can make it unsolvable. Here the player is a Booster T1 on 1 m cells, with three skills: walk straight by a given distance, turn in place and push a box. A classical planner solves the puzzle before the robot moves, the plan is checked cell by cell, and the controller carries it out one action at a time, with a bridge at every change of skill. The run below solves the puzzle in about 89 s through 23 switches.

Limitations and future work

  • The robot ends close to the target, not on it. How much of this error the incoming skill tolerates depends on the skill, and precision at the handover is the main limit.
  • The plan is kinematic and generated once. Any tracking error at the deadline is an error at the handover; training planner and tracker together, or replanning, could reduce it.
  • A new skill can be connected without training only if the corpus contains motion similar to its states. A measure of corpus coverage would tell in advance whether a switch can be trusted.
  • Target selection is replaced by a proxy. A learned estimate of where each skill succeeds would let the controller choose entry point, distance and duration.
  • All experiments run in simulation, with MuJoCo Warp through mjlab. Hardware deployment is the natural next step.
