Learning to Bridge Humanoid Robot Skills

Daniel Gigliotti1
1Sapienza University of Rome
Code

Abstract

Humanoid robots can learn agile skills one at a time, such as walking, jumping or kicking a ball, but most tasks need several of them in sequence. A learned skill only works from states similar to those it was trained on, and skills trained independently have no reason to fit together: a jump that starts from a crouch cannot take over from a robot in mid stride.

We study whether a single learned policy, which we call a bridge, can carry the robot from wherever one skill leaves it to a state from which the next one can start, while every skill stays frozen. The bridge is trained on human motion retargeted to the robot rather than on executions of the skills, and is made of a diffusion model that plans the motion of the switch and a tracking policy that executes the plan. We evaluate it in simulation on isolated switches between skills obtained in different ways, on three longer tasks, and against a direct switch and transition policies trained for each pair of skills.

Problem Definition

A skill is a policy trained on its own and then kept frozen. It succeeds only from a limited set of states, its initiation set, which depends on how it was trained. In a task, a higher level decides which skill runs next, and the switch can be requested at any moment. When it is, the robot is in a state A left by the source skill, which is usually outside the initiation set of the incoming skill.

We insert a bridge between the two skills. When the switch is requested, the bridge takes control and, within a given duration, brings the robot to a target state B from which the incoming skill can continue, then hands over. As targets we use states that the incoming skill goes through in its own runs, so that it is known to continue from them. A single state does not say how the robot is moving, so the bridge receives a short piece of motion on each side: the last second of the source motion, ending in A, and half a second of the incoming motion, starting from B, together with the duration. It does not observe the skills themselves, so the same bridge can be used for every pair.

Method

Motion Data

The bridge never sees the skills during training. It learns how the robot can move from one state to another from human motion capture, taken from LAFAN1 and from the CMU and KIT subsets of AMASS. We keep locomotion and related motions, such as walking, running, turning, stopping, crouching and jumping, selected in AMASS with the BABEL labels. The motion is retargeted to the robot with GMR, its contact with the floor is corrected, and frames the robot should not be asked to reproduce are discarded. Recorded clips rarely contain the switches the bridge has to make, so we also join pieces of unrelated clips with a motion graph, for example a walk followed by a jump. Training examples are windows cut from these clips: a history and a future, which are given to the bridge, and the motion between them, which it has to produce.

Diffusion Planner and Universal Tracker

The bridge separates deciding how the robot should move from making it move. A planner, a diffusion model trained on the motion data, receives the request and generates the whole kinematic motion between A and B at once, filling the frames between history and future by inpainting. Its denoiser is a transformer in which every frame is a token, and it predicts the changes of the root and joint positions together with the foot contacts. The resulting plan is corrected to end exactly in B.

A universal tracker, a single policy trained with PPO to follow any short kinematic trajectory, then executes the plan on the robot. It looks up to 32 control steps ahead, so near the end of the bridge it already sees the motion of the incoming skill, and its actions are corrections on top of the joint positions of the plan. The plan is generated once, when the switch is requested, and at the end of the duration control passes to the incoming skill.

Skills

Every skill is trained independently and kept frozen, and none of them was trained with the bridge in mind. They were deliberately obtained in different ways: some are trained with a task reward alone, some track a reference motion, and some combine the two.

Unitree G1

Walking follows velocity commands and is trained with a task reward. Jumping tracks a 1.54 m forward jump that starts from a crouch. Kicking tracks a human football kick, with additional rewards for hitting the ball. The four karate skills each track a single strike: oi zuki (lunge punch), mae geri (front kick), mawashi geri (roundhouse kick) and yoko geri (side kick). They are cut from one performer's fight in LAFAN1, so they all open and close in the same guard.

Booster T1

Walking moves forward by a commanded distance and stops at the destination, turning rotates in place to a commanded heading, and pushing moves a box by a given distance. All three are trained with task rewards and are used in the Sokoban task below.

Transitions

A transition isolates a single switch between two G1 skills. The same bridge is used for all of them.

Walking, Jumping and Kicking

A target state of the incoming skill is placed in front of the robot, and the bridge is called at a given distance before it, with a given duration to reach it.

Walk to jump

The jump starts from a deep crouch, and the jumping skill has never observed a walking robot. The bridge lowers the walking robot into the crouch and stops its forward motion where the jump takes off.

Walk to kick

The target lies in the approach to the ball, where the kick is already moving. The posture barely changes, but the speed and the foot on the ground have to match, and the bridge hands over a robot that is still walking.

Jump to walk

The bridge takes control while the robot is still absorbing the landing, and brings it into the walking cycle.

Karate

Each strike ends in a guard, and the next one starts from a guard held still. The target of the bridge is a state in the opening guard of the incoming strike, placed where the robot stands, and the bridge carries the robot there from the closing guard of the previous strike.

Comparison with Baselines

We compare the bridge with two baselines:

  • No-op: the incoming skill takes control as soon as the switch is requested, from whatever state the source skill left the robot in.
  • Transition policy: the method of Lee et al., Composing Complex Skills by Learning Transition Policies (ICLR 2019). It also keeps the skills frozen and inserts a learned controller between them, but a separate policy is trained for each pair of skills, on executions of the skills it connects, and the policy decides by itself when to hand over.

Walking, Jumping and Kicking

We measure how well the incoming skill performs after the handover, relative to its performance when started from its own states. With its best distance and duration, the bridge scores highest on all three switches. The difference is largest from jumping to walking, where neither baseline prepares the walk, while from walking to jumping the transition policy comes close, and from walking to kicking all three methods kick well. The bridge pays for this with time: it runs for 0.6 to 1.2 s before the handover, against at most 0.18 s for the transition policies.

Demos

In the demos a high-level controller decides which skill runs next and requests every switch, which the bridge carries out. Since the bridge only needs the request, the controller does not need to know how a switch is made.

Soccer, Unitree G1

The robot walks toward a fallen robot, jumps over it, walks to the ball and kicks it into the goal. A simple controller follows this fixed sequence and places the target of each switch with geometric rules derived from the scene, and the bridge connects the three skills while the robot keeps moving.

Karate, Unitree G1

The robot performs a routine of the four karate strikes, oi zuki, mae geri, mawashi geri and yoko geri, without being reset. After each strike, the bridge carries the robot from its closing guard into the guard from which the next strike starts.

Sokoban, Booster T1

A classical planner solves the puzzle before the robot moves, and the controller carries out the plan one action at a time with the walking, turning and pushing skills, calling the bridge at every change of skill. This run solves the puzzle in about 89 s through 23 switches. These skills all start from a robot that is nearly still, so here the bridge mostly aligns the robot, for example with the box before a push, rather than making the switch possible.
